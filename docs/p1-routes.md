# Does a weaker P1 push take a fighting route?

**Not in these recordings.** The clearest descending response even at low input is in pIP10, the courtship-related route; it grows at each step. The proposed early aggression route through pIP1 is not detected as a paired two-cell response. DNp68, sometimes proposed as a social/aggression candidate, responds much more at high input than at low input. That is the opposite of the suggested *low-drive fighting / high-drive singing* split for these named cells. It does not rule out other aggression circuits or behavior in a real fly.

P1 is a group of male brain neurons involved in social behavior. **Descending neurons** carry signals from brain to nerve cord. We compared each descending neuron's firing during the five seconds of P1 input with its firing in a matched no-input run. The numbers below are changes in spikes per second (Hz), averaged over matched runs; brackets are 95% intervals from resampling entire matched runs, not individual spikes or neurons. If a bracket includes zero, we say **no detected difference**, not “no effect.” Input was injected at one site at three Poisson input rates (10, 30 and 60 Hz), **not light intensities**. The fly does not see. This is a re-analysis of existing records, not a new simulation.

Both pIP10 cells rise at every step: high minus low is +30.90 [28.08, 32.90] Hz. The two pIP1 cells do **not** behave alike: the left cell's low change is +14.82 [-5.14, 34.90], while the right cell's is -5.56 [-8.00, -3.30] Hz. The left-cell mean alone is not evidence that the pIP1 type is recruited at low input; its interval includes zero and its random-neuron response is also large. Both DNp68 cells respond at high input, but one already shows a medium response; “high-only” would overstate the type-level result. The full cell-by-cell and type-level tables, including the random control and within-seed drive contrasts, are in the accompanying validation record.

Among named candidates, **pIP10 is the strongest reliably detected early rise**, and its response continues growing. DNp68 is a strong late riser, not an early fighting signal. Several other descending types rise late too; their labels alone cannot assign them a behavior. “Plateau” here would only mean that further change was *not detected*, never that the firing levels are equal. This analysis did not select a reliable early plateauing aggression route.

## What the fly experiments say

Hoopfer and colleagues observed that weaker optogenetic P1 stimulation enhanced inter-male aggression at a lower stimulation frequency than that needed for wing extension; the aggression often grew **after** the light was turned off rather than producing stimulus-locked lunges. Stronger stimulation gave wing extension during light and suppressed aggression then. They report pIP10 activation promoted wing extension but not aggression, and caution that these laboratory stimulation thresholds need not describe natural social behavior ([Hoopfer et al., 2015, *eLife* 4:e11346](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.11346)). Chiu and colleagues separately found that CAP neurons mediate aggressive approach and male MAP neurons control attack; they do **not** establish that the MaleCNS label DNp68 is MAP or that pIP1 is an aggression command ([Chiu et al., 2021, *Cell* 184:507–520.e16](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.11.048)). We read the full accessible articles for both sources. These findings make the question worth asking, but do not license calling any firing change here a lunge or song.

The model has no body, cannot lunge, spread wings or sing, and receives no opponent or mate sensory input. Its ON-window rates cannot test post-stimulation aggression or behavioral competition. The Poisson input rates are not equivalent to the optogenetic pulse frequencies or light powers used in flies. The observed electrical divergence is therefore **not** a reproduction of the real-fly behavioral switch.
