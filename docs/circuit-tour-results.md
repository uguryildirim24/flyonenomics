# What happened when we blocked chemicals and pushed the courtship circuit

This is a sanity check of one fixed simulated male fly brain, not a discovery about live flies. The model receives **injected** background and courtship input; it does not see, move its wings or make sound. Every change below is compared with a healthy run using the same ten starting seeds. Brackets are paired whole-seed 95% bootstrap intervals. If a bracket covers zero we say **no detected difference**, not that there is no effect.

## Chemical knockout tour

Removing acetylcholine reduced mean firing outside the directly driven sensory group by **1.436 Hz** [−1.451, −1.426] and reduced central-brain firing by **4.795 Hz** [−4.832, −4.770]. This matches the written direction of widespread silence. Removing GABA raised outside-sensory firing **12.588 Hz** [+12.563, +12.613]; removing glutamate raised it **10.779 Hz** [+10.760, +10.799]. Both match the predicted release of activity. The largest 1 ms spike fractions also rose by 0.014 and 0.011, respectively, although the GABA Fano factor **fell**, so the model did not show every predicted sign of synchronous seizure-like activity.

Photoreceptors do receive injected dark-rest input, yet the histamine block showed **no detected difference** in the optic-lobe mean (−0.001 Hz [−0.002, +0.001]). This is unclear against the frozen optic-local prediction, not evidence that histamine is unimportant in a real seeing fly. The dopamine block showed no detected difference in mean firing outside the sensory neurons (+0.001 Hz [−0.019, +0.025]). The small signed effects of the other modulators are reported in the complete analysis without treating any made-up magnitude as a pass line. Blocking the neurons without a clear chemical label lowered outside-sensory mean firing 0.130 Hz [−0.159, −0.100].

## Courtship pathway

Injecting high-rate input into the 148 male-specific P1 neurons increased their firing **32.457 Hz** [31.928, 32.837]. Two descending pIP10 neurons rose **35.940 Hz** [32.370, 38.420], and dPR1 song-circuit neurons rose **26.230 Hz** [23.390, 29.650]. Wing motor neurons rose **9.159 Hz** [8.203, 10.150]. dPR1 rose more at high than low input by **15.190 Hz** [13.180, 17.530]. At medium input P1 drove dPR1 **17.810 Hz** [15.810, 19.820] more than the matched random-neuron push. Turning on pIP10 directly raised dPR1 **14.410 Hz** [11.360, 17.830], without requiring a P1 push. These changes follow the principal written expectation that the signal can reach the song circuit.

There are clear limits: vPR6 never spiked in these runs despite receiving an anatomically mapped route, so **not every** named song neuron responded. Raw first-spike order does **not** cleanly match the written sequence: wing motor neurons often fired spontaneously at the start of the window, before the injected P1 response. The five-second OFF means are near healthy rest for many named groups, but these records do not resolve the prediction of return **within one second**. The model has no body or slow arousal mechanism; these are spikes, not courtship or song.
